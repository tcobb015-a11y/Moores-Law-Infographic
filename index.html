Moore's Law

How thousands of transistors became billions

The basic idea

In 1965, Gordon Moore observed that the number of components on integrated circuits was increasing rapidly. He later revised the pace to roughly a doubling every two years.

WHY IT MATTERS

The Growth Is Exponential, Not Linear

If every transistor were represented at its true scale, the newest bar would be impossibly large. This comparison uses a logarithmic scale so the milestones can fit on one screen.

≈ 8.7 million ×

The 2022 Apple M2 contains about 8.7 million times as many transistors as Intel's 1971 4004.

THE BIGGER PICTURE

More Transistors Changed What Computers Could Do

01

More Complexity

More transistors allow designers to build increasingly complex functions into chips.

02

Smaller Technology

Advances in manufacturing helped put more computing capability into smaller devices.

03

A Technology Target

Moore's observation became an influential benchmark for the semiconductor industry.

Important context: Moore's Law is an observation and industry benchmark, not a law of physics. Modern chips also differ greatly in architecture. The M2, for example, is a system-on-a-chip, so this timeline is meant to illustrate the enormous scale of transistor growth rather than claim that every chip is directly equivalent.
